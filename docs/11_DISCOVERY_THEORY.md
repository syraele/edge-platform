# Discovery Theory

Version: 1.0

Status: Foundational

---

## Purpose

This document defines the theoretical basis for determining whether a new Knowledge item represents a genuine discovery.

It does not describe implementation, algorithms, or technical procedures.
It defines the conceptual criteria that will govern the Intelligence phase of EDGE_ENGINE.

The central question is the following:

When can the system say that it has discovered something it did not previously know?

---

## 1. What counts as a discovery?

A discovery is not simply the creation of a new statement.
A discovery occurs when a new Knowledge item adds meaningful, non-redundant understanding to the system's accumulated knowledge of the market.

In other words, a discovery is a gain in explanatory or predictive content.

A new Knowledge item is a genuine discovery only if it introduces a difference that is significant at the level of understanding.

That difference may be one of the following:

- it reveals a new phenomenon;
- it refines an existing phenomenon with higher precision;
- it establishes a previously unrecognized relationship between known phenomena;
- it changes the system's understanding of the conditions under which a phenomenon appears.

A discovery therefore means an increase in the system's conceptual knowledge, not merely the arrival of a new observation.

---

## 2. When a Knowledge is only a variant of something already known

A new Knowledge item is a variant of an already known one when it does not add a new conceptual distinction.

This happens when the new item:

- expresses the same underlying regularity with different wording;
- describes the same phenomenon under a different formulation;
- uses a different but equivalent observable condition;
- adds only stylistic or descriptive variation without modifying understanding.

In such cases, the new Knowledge does not increase the system's knowledge in a meaningful way.
It is a reformulation, not a discovery.

Theoretical criterion:

A Knowledge item is not a discovery if it is conceptually subsumed by an existing body of knowledge.

In that case, the system should recognize it as a variant, an instance, or a re-expression of what it already knows.

---

## 3. When a Knowledge represents an refinement of an existing phenomenon

A new Knowledge item represents a refinement when it does not create a wholly new phenomenon, but improves the system's understanding of an existing one.

This occurs when the new item:

- narrows the conditions under which the phenomenon appears;
- improves the precision of its description;
- clarifies its direction, timing, magnitude, or context;
- distinguishes subtypes of a known phenomenon;
- identifies a more specific boundary or regime in which the phenomenon is valid.

In such cases, the new Knowledge is not a completely new discovery in the strong sense, but it is still a meaningful advancement.

It increases the quality of the system's understanding of a known phenomenon.

Theoretical criterion:

A Knowledge item is a refinement when it preserves the identity of an existing phenomenon but improves its conceptual precision, scope, or explanatory power.

This is a genuine intellectual advance, even if it does not introduce an entirely new phenomenon.

---

## 4. When a Knowledge represents a genuinely new phenomenon

A new Knowledge item represents a genuinely new phenomenon when it introduces a new regularity that is not reducible to an existing one.

This occurs when the new item:

- identifies a pattern that is not already represented by the system's current conceptual model;
- reveals a new causal or structural relationship;
- describes a regularity that cannot be understood as a mere variant or refinement of a known phenomenon;
- changes the system's map of what the market can do.

A true discovery is not simply the addition of a new instance of an old pattern.
It is the recognition of a new conceptual object.

Theoretical criterion:

A Knowledge item is a genuine discovery when it introduces a new phenomenon, that is, a regularity that is not fully contained within existing conceptual categories.

This is the strongest form of discovery.

---

## 5. Theoretical criteria for calling something a discovery

For the system to say, "I have discovered something that I did not previously know," several conditions must be satisfied.

### 1. Non-redundancy

The new Knowledge must not be fully redundant with existing knowledge.
It must contain a meaningful difference in conceptual content.

### 2. Conceptual addition

The new knowledge must add something to the system's understanding.
It may add a new phenomenon, a new distinction within an existing phenomenon, or a new relation between known structures.

### 3. Empirical grounding

The new Knowledge must be grounded in evidence.
A discovery is not an intuition, a metaphor, or an untested label.
It must be connected to observation.

### 4. Relevance to the research domain

The new Knowledge must matter within the domain of inquiry.
It must contribute to the system's ability to understand market behavior.

### 5. Stability of meaning

The new Knowledge must be sufficiently clear and coherent to stand as a stable contribution.
A merely unstable or ambiguous statement cannot count as a genuine discovery.

### 6. Recognition of novelty

The system must be able to distinguish between "this is a variation of what I already know" and "this introduces something genuinely new." 

Novelty is therefore not a matter of novelty in appearance, but novelty in conceptual content.

---

## 6. The distinction between novelty and mere difference

Not every difference is a discovery.

A new Knowledge item may differ from an existing one in form, wording, context, or measurement, yet still express the same underlying conception.
That is not a discovery.

A discovery requires more than difference.
It requires a difference that matters.

The important distinction is:

- difference of expression is not enough;
- difference of conceptual content is necessary.

This principle is central to the theory of discovery.

---

## 7. The role of the system in recognizing discovery

EDGE_ENGINE does not create discovery by declaring something new.
It recognizes discovery by identifying a gain in understanding that is both substantive and grounded.

The system's role is therefore not to invent novelty, but to detect it.

Discovery is a property of the relationship between:

- what the system already knows;
- what the new evidence suggests;
- what conceptual structure the new Knowledge introduces.

A discovery is recognized when the system can say that the new Knowledge cannot be fully accounted for by the existing conceptual framework.

---

## 8. The theoretical foundation of Intelligence

The Intelligence phase must be guided by one core principle:

A new Knowledge is a true discovery only if it increases the system's understanding in a way that is not already contained in its existing knowledge structure.

This principle yields three conceptual categories:

1. Variant
   - same underlying meaning, different form.

2. Refinement
   - same phenomenon, improved precision or scope.

3. New phenomenon
   - a genuinely new regularity not previously represented.

These three categories are the foundation of the discovery theory.
They define the boundary between redundancy, improvement, and genuine novelty.

---

## 9. Final definition

EDGE_ENGINE can say that it has discovered something new when a new Knowledge item satisfies the following theoretical condition:

It introduces a meaningful and evidence-grounded increase in understanding that is not fully contained in the system's existing knowledge structure.

That increase may take the form of:

- a new phenomenon;
- a refinement of an existing phenomenon;
- a previously unrecognized relation between phenomena.

If the new Knowledge does not satisfy that condition, then it is not a discovery in the strong sense.
It is at most a variant, a restatement, or an instance of what was already known.
